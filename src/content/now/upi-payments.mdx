<p>
  UPI launched in April 2016 with 21 pilot banks and a simple promise: move money
  between any two bank accounts, instantly, for free, using just a phone. In its
  first full year it processed 0.18 billion transactions. By FY 2024–25 the
  annual figure was around <strong>185 billion</strong> — the largest real-time
  payments system in the world, ahead of China's, and by some measures
  processing more daily transactions than Visa.
</p>

<p>
  Three things made this curve possible, and none of them is an app. First,
  <strong>Jan Dhan</strong> put a bank account within reach of every household.
  Second, <strong>Aadhaar</strong> solved identity verification cheaply at
  population scale. Third, <strong>NPCI</strong> — a not-for-profit created in
  2008 — owned the rail itself, so every bank and every wallet could connect to
  every other one instead of building silos. UPI is the layer where those
  foundations met.
</p>

<p>
  The policy choices matter as much as the plumbing: the decision to scrap
  merchant fees (MDR) in 2019 made sub-₹200 transactions viable, and UPI's
  open, interoperable design is precisely why Brazil, the UAE, Singapore and
  others have since licensed or cloned it. When people say India "went
  cashless like China," the correction is sharper: China's wallets grew inside
  two corporate ecosystems. India built a <em>public</em> rail that any company
  or bank can ride.
</p>

<p class="text-sm text-fog italic">
  Figures for FY23 and earlier are as reported in India's Economic Survey and
  NPCI statistics; FY24–FY25 figures are from NPCI's annual reporting and are
  rounded. Monthly figures (e.g., August 2025: 20 billion transactions) show
  the annualised pace continuing to grow.
</p>
