## Where I saw it

NASA's Artemis programme and China's crewed lunar ambitions both work
backward from a dated landing target through a funded sequence of
intermediate missions — a space station, a lander, a return vehicle, a
resource survey — each de-risking the next. A goal without that sequence
tends to slip indefinitely; a goal with it tends to happen. India's own
lunar ambition needs the same discipline, across all three phases a real
lunar programme actually has: landing, resource use, and a permanent
outpost.

## What's missing

India has stated an ambition to land an astronaut on the Moon, and
Chandrayaan-3 already proved precision landing and found evidence of
water-ice near the south pole — but no country, including India, has yet
turned that kind of discovery into a working resource-extraction
demonstrator, let alone a permanently staffed base. What's missing is the
published, funded three-phase sequence that connects where India already
is to where no nation has gone yet.

## What India already has

- **Gaganyaan**, India's human-spaceflight programme, already in
  development.
- **Chandrayaan-3's precision landing** near the lunar south pole, and
  the water-ice evidence found in that exact region.
- **The Bharatiya Antariksh Station**, planned for the mid-2030s, a
  natural staging point for lunar hardware.
- **India's deep materials-processing and chemical-engineering base**,
  relevant to designing a compact in-situ resource extraction unit.
- **India's Antarctic and Arctic research stations**, decades of
  experience running permanently staffed outposts in extreme, isolated
  environments — the closest Earth-based analogue to a lunar base.

## The proposal

**Phase 1 — Landing.** Publish the intermediate mission sequence —
uncrewed lunar lander test, crewed lunar flyby, then landing — each with
its own funded milestone, using the Bharatiya Antariksh Station as a
staging and testing point for lunar hardware, and building the heavy-lift
launch capability a crewed mission needs as its own tracked programme.

**Phase 2 — Resources.** Once landing capability is proven, commission a
small lunar in-situ resource utilisation (ISRU) demonstrator targeting
the same south-pole region, to extract and measure water from the
regolith — the step between discovery and resource that no country has
completed yet. Design it explicitly for refuelling and life-support
applications, so it feeds directly into Phase 3.

**Phase 3 — Outpost.** Design a permanently staffed lunar base around
the ISRU demonstrator's findings, so water extraction is built into the
first module rather than added later. Start with short-duration crewed
rotations and extend gradually, the same phased approach India's polar
stations already use, with international partnership on science modules
while India leads the habitat and life-support core.

Throughout: **invite international partnership selectively**, only on
tasks that don't compromise India's own capability-building, and
**publish a decades-long but concrete staffing and expansion plan**, so
each phase is a funded commitment, not a slogan.

Ask: ISRO to publish a single funded, dated roadmap spanning all three
phases — landing, resource use, and permanent outpost — rather than
treating each as a separate, disconnected ambition.
