## Where I saw it

The US and Germany now 3D-print flight-critical parts for aircraft
engines and defence hardware at production volumes, not just prototypes —
metal additive manufacturing has moved from a design tool to a
manufacturing method for parts too complex or too low-volume for
traditional tooling to make economically.

## What's missing

India's additive-manufacturing use is concentrated in prototyping and
tooling, with limited production-grade metal 3D printing for aerospace,
defence or automotive parts. Given India's own fighter-engine, AMCA and
defence-manufacturing ambitions already proposed, qualified additive
manufacturing is a capability those programmes will need directly.

## What India already has

- **A growing base of Indian 3D-printing companies and research
  institutions**, already working in polymer and some metal printing.
- **DRDO and HAL's component-manufacturing needs**, a ready customer base
  for qualified production-grade additive parts.
- **India's metallurgy and materials-science research base**, relevant to
  qualifying new printable alloys.

## The proposal

1. **A metal additive-manufacturing qualification programme**, so
   printed parts can be certified for use in aerospace and defence
   hardware, not just prototypes.
2. **Tie it directly to the fighter-engine and AMCA programmes**, as a
   way to manufacture complex, low-volume parts those programmes need.
3. **Shared industrial printing facilities** for smaller manufacturers
   who can't justify their own equipment.
4. **Materials research funding** for domestically qualified printable
   alloys, reducing reliance on imported printing feedstock.

Ask: the Ministry of Heavy Industries and DRDO to fund a metal additive-
manufacturing qualification programme tied to India's aerospace and
defence production needs.
