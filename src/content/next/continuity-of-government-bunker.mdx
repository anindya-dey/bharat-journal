## Where I saw it

The US, the UK and Russia each maintain hardened, below-ground continuity-
of-government facilities — built to keep core decision-making functioning
through a disaster or attack that disables the capital itself. These
sites are rarely discussed publicly, but their existence is treated as a
basic insurance policy by every major power that has one.

## What's missing

The satellite-capitals proposal already in this journal distributes
load across surface cities for everyday resilience. What it doesn't
cover is the harder, lower-probability but higher-consequence scenario a
hardened site exists for — a below-ground or deep-mountain facility
specifically designed so continuity of government survives even if a
capital region is temporarily unusable.

## What India already has

- **The underground research cities already proposed**, sharing much of
  the deep-construction and life-support engineering a hardened
  government site would need.
- **NDMA's existing disaster-continuity planning**, an institutional
  home for extending contingency planning to this scenario specifically.
- **India's mountain geology**, offering secure, stable sites away from
  the most exposed coastal and border regions.

## The proposal

1. **One hardened, below-ground continuity-of-government site**,
   distinct from the satellite-capitals' surface cities, designed for the
   lowest-probability, highest-consequence scenario specifically.
2. **Build on the underground-research-cities engineering programme**,
   rather than commissioning unrelated, one-off construction expertise.
3. **Regular, quiet continuity exercises**, the way other countries with
   such sites rehearse the handover without public disclosure of
   operational details.
4. **Treat it as infrastructure, not announcement** — the existence of
   the capability matters more than its publicised specifics.

Ask: the Cabinet Secretariat and NDMA to commission a hardened continuity-
of-government site, built on the underground-research-cities engineering
programme.
