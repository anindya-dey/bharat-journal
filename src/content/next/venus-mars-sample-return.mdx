## Where I saw it

NASA and ESA's joint Mars Sample Return effort and Japan's Hayabusa
missions, which successfully brought asteroid material back to Earth,
represent the hardest tier of deep-space missions — not just reaching
another world, but leaving it again with material in hand. Only a handful
of sample-return missions, from a handful of agencies, have ever
succeeded.

## What's missing

India reached Mars orbit on its first attempt and is developing a Venus
orbiter, both genuine achievements on a fraction of comparable missions'
budgets. Neither mission returns material to Earth. The opportunity is to
set sample return as the explicit next tier of ambition, rather than
orbiting indefinitely.

## What India already has

- **Mangalyaan**, which reached Mars orbit on its first attempt.
- **Shukrayaan**, India's planned Venus orbiter mission, in development.
- **Chandrayaan-3's precision landing**, proof of the guidance and control
  technology a sample-return mission would need.

## The proposal

1. **A funded feasibility study for a Mars or Venus sample-return
   mission**, building on Mangalyaan and Shukrayaan's groundwork.
2. **Start with a lunar sample-return mission as the proving ground**,
   since the Moon is a far less demanding return trip than Mars or Venus.
3. **Build the re-entry and recovery capability** this requires as its
   own tracked technology line, shared with the human-spaceflight
   programme's capsule work.
4. **International partnership on the hardest sub-systems only**, keeping
   the core mission Indian-led.

Ask: ISRO to commission a feasibility study for a lunar sample-return
mission as the first step toward Mars or Venus.
