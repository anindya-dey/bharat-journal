## Where I saw it

Mission Shakti, India's 2019 anti-satellite test, destroyed a target
satellite in low-earth orbit and made India one of only a handful of
nations to demonstrate the capability at all. What separates a
demonstration from a deterrent is repeatability — a standing fleet of
interceptors, kinetic and non-kinetic, ready on short notice, rather than
a single test fired once and retired.

## What's missing

India proved the hardest part — hit-to-kill accuracy against an orbital
target — but has not published a programme to turn that one test into a
standing, multi-interceptor capability. The tri-service space command
already proposed in this journal needs an actual fleet to command, not
just an elevated budget line.

## What India already has

- **Mission Shakti (2019)**, India's demonstrated kinetic anti-satellite
  capability.
- **DRDO's Ballistic Missile Defence interceptors**, which share much of
  the guidance and interception technology an orbital interceptor fleet
  would need.
- **The space-situational-awareness network already proposed**, the
  exact tracking layer an interceptor fleet depends on to find its
  targets.

## The proposal

1. **A standing interceptor fleet**, mixing kinetic interceptors with
   non-kinetic options — jamming and dazzling — so every response isn't a
   debris-generating kinetic strike.
2. **Tie it directly to the space-situational-awareness network**, so
   target tracking and interception are one integrated system.
3. **Publish a responsible-use doctrine**, distinguishing deterrent
   readiness from any offensive first-use posture, to manage the debris
   and escalation risks explicit in this capability.
4. **Regular testing against decommissioned, not operational, targets**,
   to maintain readiness without repeating the debris created in 2019.

Ask: DRDO and the Defence Space Agency to publish a funded roadmap from
Mission Shakti's single test to a standing, doctrine-governed interceptor
fleet.
