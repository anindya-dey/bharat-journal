## Where I saw it

A crewed Mars mission's hardest unsolved problem may not be propulsion
but biology: months in transit expose a crew to radiation, muscle and
bone loss, and psychological strain that current countermeasures only
partly address. Research into induced torpor or medically managed
long-duration stasis — reducing metabolic demand for much of the
journey — has been studied in early research settings by a few space
agencies, but no country has a working solution. It sits alongside Mars
terraforming as a genuinely open scientific frontier.

## What's missing

India's Mars-terraforming research proposal already in this journal
treats the destination's habitability as open scientific territory.
The journey itself is just as open — and India's medical-research and
space programmes have never been combined to work on it.

## What India already has

- **India's medical-research institutions**, including deep expertise in
  critical-care and metabolic medicine directly relevant to induced-
  torpor research.
- **ISRO's human-spaceflight programme (Gaganyaan)**, providing the
  space-medicine context this research would eventually feed into.
- **DBT-funded biomedical research networks**, an existing structure to
  house a long-horizon physiology research line.

## The proposal

1. **A joint ISRO-DBT research programme** into induced-torpor and
   long-duration stasis physiology, framed explicitly as basic,
   multi-decade research.
2. **Start with animal and short-duration human studies**, in
   partnership with India's critical-care medical research community.
3. **Publish openly and seek international academic collaboration**,
   since no country currently leads this field competitively.
4. **Treat it as a talent magnet for space medicine**, the same way deep-
   space missions already draw planetary scientists to Indian
   institutions.

Ask: ISRO and the Department of Biotechnology to jointly fund early-stage
long-duration human stasis research for future deep-space missions.
