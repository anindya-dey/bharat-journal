## Where I saw it

Israel operates armed and sensor-laden unmanned ground vehicles along
sections of its border fences, handling patrol and surveillance in
terrain where posting soldiers around the clock is costly and dangerous.
Separately, US military research into powered exosuits aims to let
infantry carry heavier loads over longer distances with less fatigue and
fewer injuries. Different machines, same principle: let technology take
the worst of what the terrain demands, so soldiers don't have to carry
it alone.

## What's missing

India's Himalayan and high-altitude borders are among the most
physically punishing deployed terrain in the world — extreme cold, low
oxygen, and difficult resupply. That is exactly the environment where
both unmanned ground vehicles and load-bearing exoskeletons do the most
good, and where India has fielded the least capability relative to its
geography, in either category.

## What India already has

- **DRDO's unmanned ground vehicle research**, including robotic mules
  and surveillance platforms already demonstrated for high-altitude use.
- **DRDO's robotics and biomechanics research programmes**, a starting
  base for exosuit development.
- **Decades of high-altitude logistics and cold-weather-gear
  experience** — the world's highest battlefield infrastructure already
  exists; both technologies extend it.
- **India's robotics and drone startup ecosystem**, growing quickly
  since the 2021 Drone Rules.

## The proposal

1. **Fielded autonomous sentry and resupply robots** for the highest-
   altitude, lowest-density border posts first, with AI-assisted
   surveillance so a single human operator can monitor a wide sector
   rather than walk it.
2. **A load-bearing exoskeleton pilot** for the same high-altitude
   resupply and patrol troops, focused on reducing injury and fatigue
   rather than full combat augmentation.
3. **Cold-weather power and hardening as one shared research track** for
   both technologies, since extreme-altitude power density and cold-
   weather durability are harder problems than temperate-climate designs
   solve for, and solving them once serves both programmes.
4. **A phased human-machine handover doctrine**, so robots and exosuits
   take the routine and physical load while decisions stay with
   soldiers, field-tested with troops already deployed at extreme
   altitude rather than developed in a lab alone.

Ask: DRDO and the Army to commission a fielded pilot of both autonomous
sentry robots and a load-bearing exoskeleton on one high-altitude sector,
sharing the cold-weather power research between them.
